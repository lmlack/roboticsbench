Watch out

        
    
          
  • ROS messages, tf2 and SciPy store quaternions x y z w. Eigen's constructor takes w, x, y, z, but .coeffs() returns x y z w.
    • 
          
  • URDF rpy is roll, pitch, yaw about fixed axes: extrinsic XYZ, which equals intrinsic ZYX with the angles reversed.
    • 
          
  • q and -q are the same rotation. This page keeps w ≥ 0; other tools may not, so compare rotations, not raw numbers.
    • 
          
  • SciPy: uppercase sequence letters are intrinsic, lowercase are extrinsic. "XYZ" and "xyz" are different rotations.
    • 
          
  • Inputs accept pi, sqrt() and arithmetic, so pi/2 or sqrt(2)/2 work.

What URDF is
and what reads it

        

          

            
The format

            
An XML file describing one robot as rigid links joined by joints. A link carries three optional bodies: visual for what you see, collision for what the planner tests against, usually a coarser shape, and inertial for mass and the inertia tensor.

            
The joints form a tree: one root link, and every other link reached by exactly one joint from its parent. That is the format's main limit. A closed loop, like a delta robot or a four-bar linkage, cannot be written down directly. mimic couples one joint to another and covers some of it; past that you need SDF or a solver that closes the loop itself.

          

          

            
What a joint says

            
origin places the child frame in the parent at zero position, axis is the unit vector it turns or slides along, and limit caps travel, effort and velocity.

            
Six types, three of them common: revolute turns within limits, continuous turns forever, like a wheel, prismatic slides. fixed moves not at all and is how sensors, tool plates and named frames get attached. floating and planar exist and are thinly supported.

            
Meters, radians and kilograms throughout. rpy is fixed-axis roll, pitch, yaw, applied X then Y then Z, which is the same rotation as intrinsic Z-Y-X. Frames are right handed, and REP 103 asks for x forward, y left, z up.

          

          

            
Where it gets used

            
It is the one description a whole ROS stack shares, so the pieces agree on the geometry:

            
    
              
  • robot_state_publisher takes the URDF plus live joint angles and publishes the TF tree, which is how anything else answers "where is the camera relative to the gripper right now".
    • 
              
  • RViz draws the robot from it. Gazebo and other simulators take the inertial and collision bodies as the physics, with extra gazebo tags for sensors and plugins.
    • 
              
  • MoveIt plans against the joint limits and collision bodies, with a companion SRDF adding the meaning: which joints form an arm, what counts as the end effector, which link pairs never collide.
    • 
              
  • KDL, Pinocchio and friends build forward kinematics, inverse kinematics, Jacobians and dynamics from the same file. This page does the first and third of those.
    • 
            

          

          

            
How they are really written

            
Almost nobody types URDF by hand. It comes out of a CAD exporter, or it is written as xacro, an XML macro language with variables, arithmetic and includes, and expanded into plain URDF when the robot launches. Raw URDF has no way to reuse anything, so a six-jointed arm would be six copies of the same block with the numbers changed.

            
Meshes are referenced as package://my_robot_description/meshes/link1.stl, a path resolved against the installed ROS packages rather than the disk. Nothing outside ROS knows how to follow that, which is why this viewer asks for the folder: it matches the tail of the path against the files you handed it.

Localization

    
    
A robot works out where it is from noisy odometry and range and bearing to known landmarks, with walls in the way. Five estimators read the same data, plus a sixth, SLAM, that has to build the map as it goes. Turn on the wind to push the robot in a way none of them is told about.

  


  

    

      
      
      
      
      
      
    

    

      How do they differ?
      
    
        
  • Particle filter: many sampled guesses at the pose. Guesses that explain the measurements survive resampling, so it can hold several at once.
    • 
        
  • EKF: one Gaussian, updated by linearizing the motion and measurement models.
    • 
        
  • UKF: one Gaussian, carried through the models by seven sigma points instead of a linearization.
    • 
        
  • EKF-SLAM: an EKF that is never given the map. It adds each landmark the first time it sees one, then improves the robot and the whole map together. Its faded crosses and ellipses are the map it has built.
    • 
        
  • Grid: a probability for every 25 cm cell and 22.5° heading. Slow to update, but it can hold any shape of belief.
    • 
        
  • Dead reckoning: adds up the odometry and ignores the landmarks.
    •

Motion model

        

        

        
The estimators get the commanded v and ω. The real robot executes them with this noise added, and a wall can stop it while the command says it moved.

      

      

        
Measurement model

        

        

        
Only landmarks within the max range are measured, and only if the straight line from the robot to the landmark crosses no wall. Each measurement says which landmark it came from.

      

      

        
Dead reckoning

        

        

        
No measurement step. Every bit of slip and every bump stays in the estimate for good, so the error only grows.

      

      

        
Particle filter, every step

        

        

        

      

      

        
Grid Bayes filter

        

        

        
Cells inside walls are held at zero. Motion that would carry belief into a wall leaves it where it was.

RRT* rewiring radius
